Anchor IDs refer to the private timestamped transcript; the public release contains paraphrases, not the raw transcript. ## Purpose and audience

The introduction should help a reader decide, early and accurately, why the study matters and how to interpret the rest of the manuscript. It brings a reader from the broader world into the study, sets the scene, and supplies a roadmap; methodological rigor cannot compensate for choosing or positioning an unimportant problem. [T000087–T000097; visual cross-check at 00:09:20]

The reader should be able to infer from the introduction what the authors are trying to achieve, why the study is justified, and broadly how they respond to the problem. The paper should then deliver the same central story through the conclusion. [T000023–T000027]

## Persuasive introduction logic

Use the following reasoning sequence as a diagnostic model, not a mandatory paragraph count:

1. **Who cares?** Establish a consequential phenomenon, problem, or unresolved theoretical tension for the target scholarly audience; add practitioner relevance when the outlet and study warrant it. [T000103–T000105]
2. **What conversation are we joining?** Identify what the relevant, current literature already explains and where that conversation has reached. [T000106–T000109; T000210–T000217]
3. **What remains unresolved, and why does that matter?** Show the consequence of the unknown, conflict, assumption, boundary failure, or changed condition. Mere absence is not enough. [T000106–T000114]
4. **What will this study do?** State the research response clearly enough that readers understand its purpose and broad approach. [T000025–T000027; T000217]
5. **What will readers learn?** Preview the difference the study can make to cumulative knowledge, calibrated to the actual design and anticipated evidence. [T000111–T000114; T000505–T000509]

A phenomenon-driven opening and a theory-driven opening are both legitimate. One panelist personally found consequential real-world phenomena more engaging, especially in an applied field; treat that as a stated preference, not a universal rule. [T000151–T000160]

## Research-problem and gap tests

### Missingness is not importance

Reject a gap that amounts only to “prior studies have not examined X.” An unstudied topic may simply be unimportant. Require an explanation of why resolving it changes understanding or action. [T000003–T000007; T000110–T000114]

### Rigor cannot rescue the wrong question

Do not confuse a technically elaborate design with an important project. Test whether the problem is consequential before praising the number of studies, methods, or robustness checks. [T000092–T000097]

### Prefer consequential uncertainty

A promising problem often lacks an obvious one-sided answer, contains a genuine tension, or has become unsettled because the macro-environment changed. An intuitively settled question needs a strong reason to be reopened. [T000135–T000140]

### Context must alter the explanation

A new country, culture, industry, technology, or tourism setting is not automatically a contribution. Ask which property of the setting challenges an assumed mechanism, limits prior generalization, changes a relationship, or reveals a boundary condition. Explain why that difference matters to the target journal's audience. [T000112–T000114; T000177–T000186]

### Establish importance through disciplined exposure

The panelists recommended sustained reading across tourism and relevant parent disciplines, discussion with scholars and practitioners, conferences, and early feedback as ways to test whether a problem is timely and worth investigating. [T000119–T000125; T000127–T000140]

## Contribution types and credibility tests

### Novelty is not contribution

Something can be new or exciting without adding meaningful knowledge. Ask whether the work extends, challenges, integrates, qualifies, or otherwise changes what the field can understand. [T000014–T000022; T000097–T000102]

### Fewer defensible claims beat a catalogue

Do not label the same paper contextual, theoretical, methodological, and practical merely to appear competitive. Identify the core contribution or small set of contributions that the evidence can genuinely support; do not promise more in the introduction than the completed paper delivers. [T000029–T000037]

### Findings are not yet contributions

Findings report what the analysis produced. Contributions explain how those findings change cumulative knowledge. Require an explicit interpretation of the difference made, rather than relabelling a result as a contribution. [T000259–T000265]

### Plausible contribution mechanisms

Depending on the design and evidence, a defensible contribution may include:

- defining a construct or developing a justified way to measure an emerging phenomenon; [T000241–T000245]
- connecting previously separate but conceptually related constructs; [T000245–T000247]
- revising the form of an established relationship, such as showing that it is not linear; [T000247–T000249]
- explaining an underlying process or identifying when an effect holds and fails; [T000249–T000258]
- integrating complementary theoretical perspectives into a necessary framework; [T000474–T000493]
- making a strong, relevant knowledge contribution without claiming to overturn a mature parent theory. [T000439–T000461]

This list is not a licence to assign labels. Each claim still needs a demonstrated change relative to prior knowledge.

### Weak methodological claim

Changing from a previously used quantitative design to mixed methods does not, by itself, establish a methodological contribution. A method belongs in the contribution claim only when its innovation materially changes what can be known or how a persistent problem can be investigated. [T000223–T000240]

### Theory count is subordinate to explanatory need

One panelist's practical preference was one, or at most two, overarching theories for the manuscript while allowing relevant theory to justify individual links among constructs. Treat this as a design heuristic, not a journal rule. [T000531–T000541]

## Paragraph- and move-level guidance

Every sentence should have a purpose; every paragraph should communicate a message and help the reader transition to the next message. Diagnose the introduction as a road map, not as a pile of individually correct paragraphs. [T000285–T000300]

A common workable progression is context or phenomenon, relevant theory and conversation, what is explained and unresolved, the research response, and a calibrated contribution preview. Adapt it to the study rather than forcing a fixed template. [T000277–T000284]

Use citations for argumentative work: to establish problem importance, demonstrate currency, identify the relevant conversation, and support the transition to the unresolved issue. Recent scholarly work, credible industry or government reports, and current statistics may support different kinds of claims. Choose citations by function, not by a target count. [T000194–T000220]

When the paper contains a separate literature review, keep the introduction selective and readable; place detailed literature development in the review. This was presented as a panelist preference, and manuscript structures vary. [T000194–T000200]

Drafting is iterative. After completing the manuscript, return to the introduction and verify that it actually sets up the paper that was written. After revision, remove duplicated or bolted-on material that reviewer requests may have introduced. A fresh reader or co-author pass is valuable because authors know their own intended logic too well. [T000301–T000338]

## Introduction versus discussion contribution claims

In the introduction, preview the main difference the paper intends to make concisely—one focused paragraph, or at most two, was suggested as a practical heuristic. In the discussion or conclusion, use the actual results to explain each supported contribution in detail, including why it matters and how it affects theory or practice. Bullet-point labels without interpretation are insufficient. [T000495–T000527]

## Strong and weak examples

These examples are newly written applications of the lecture logic, not quotations from the webinar.

### Contextual gap

- Weak: “No study has tested model M among visitors in country X.”
- Stronger when evidenced: “Model M assumes that cue C carries the same meaning across settings. In country X, institution I changes that cue's meaning, creating a consequential test of the mechanism and its boundary.”

### Novel technology

- Weak: “Technology T is new in tourism, so studying adoption is novel.”
- Stronger when evidenced: “Technology T changes the user's agency in a way that conflicts with the mechanism assumed by adoption theory; the study tests the revised mechanism and conditions under which it holds.”

### Method claim

- Weak: “Prior studies were quantitative; using mixed methods is a methodological contribution.”
- Stronger when evidenced: “The established measure cannot represent process P. The new design observes P directly and demonstrates how that changes inference.”

### Contribution calibration

- Weak: “This study makes theoretical, methodological, contextual, and practical contributions.”
- Stronger: name the one or two differences that the results can defend, then state practical implications separately without inflating them into theory.

## Common failure modes

- treating an empty cell in the literature as sufficient justification; [T000003–T000007]
- relying on rigor or complexity instead of problem importance; [T000092–T000097]
- presenting a new setting or technology as contribution without a changed mechanism or boundary; [T000014–T000022; T000177–T000186]
- listing every possible contribution type and overpromising; [T000029–T000037]
- confusing findings with the difference they make to knowledge; [T000259–T000265]
- counting citations rather than assigning them argumentative functions; [T000205–T000220]
- losing the central storyline after revisions by appending repetitive material; [T000319–T000338]
- repeating contribution labels in the discussion without explaining why or how they matter. [T000520–T000527]

## Q&A clarification on generative AI

The panel expressed different degrees of caution, but converged on author responsibility. AI may be used as an assistant to cross-check reasoning, spot inconsistencies, refine clarity, or correct language while the author remains in control of ideas and evidence. One panelist objected to using it to create the initial draft or assemble substantive content; another stressed preserving an identifiable human voice. Publisher and journal policies must be checked independently because these comments are personal guidance, not formal policy. [T000354–T000390; T000392–T000415]

Operational rule for this skill: never invent study facts, literature, findings, or contribution claims; expose every substantive addition for author review; preserve the author's evidential boundaries and voice.
